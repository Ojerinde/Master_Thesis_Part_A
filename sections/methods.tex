\section{Methods}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Corpus construction}
\label{sec:corpus}

We use the static family of the Texas Spoofing Test Battery
\citep{humphreys2012texbat}: the clean static recording as the authentic baseline and
the ds2, ds3 and ds7 spoofing scenarios. ds2 is an overpowered time push at about
10 dB advantage, ds3 is a matched-power time push at about 1.3 dB, and ds7 is a
power-matched, carrier-phase-aligned takeover \citep{humphreys2016texbatds78}. The
benchmark's moving-platform scenarios, ds5 and ds6, fall outside the stationary-receiver
scope of this study. Each recording is processed by the receiver of
Section~\ref{sec:signal} into per-epoch observables.

Labels are assigned within each recording rather than by whole-recording identity: in a
spoofed recording the epochs before the documented takeover carry the authentic label
and the epochs after the pull-off carry the spoofed label, with the transition band
discarded; the clean static recording is authentic throughout. Drawing both classes
from the same recordings removes the recording-identity shortcut, under which a
detector could separate the classes by learning which recording an epoch came from
rather than the spoofing signature. The resulting corpus holds about 209{,}000 labelled
epochs, close to balanced (about 51\% spoofed), so no resampling is applied.

Train, validation and test partitions are formed by a leakage-free block-temporal
split. Within each satellite and recording segment the epochs are ordered by time and
cut into contiguous blocks of 70\%, 10\% and 20\%, with a purge gap dropped at each
boundary. A random per-epoch split would leak, because adjacent epochs of the same
satellite are near-duplicates through temporal autocorrelation, so a random split lets
almost-identical epochs fall on both sides of the partition and inflates the reported
performance.

\subsection{Detectors}
\label{sec:detectors}

We evaluate fourteen detectors. The eight classical models are a random forest
\citep{breiman2001random}, gradient boosting \citep{friedman2001greedy}, XGBoost
\citep{chen2016xgboost}, LightGBM \citep{ke2017lightgbm}, a $k$-nearest-neighbour
classifier, a multilayer perceptron, a decision tree and a support-vector machine with a
radial-basis-function kernel, tuned by the same five-fold grid search as every other
classical model. The six deep models are a one-dimensional convolutional network, a long
short-term memory network \citep{hochreiter1997long}, a bidirectional LSTM, a
convolutional-recurrent hybrid, a Transformer \citep{vaswani2017attention} and a temporal
convolutional network \citep{bai2018empirical}. The multilayer perceptron is a shallow
feed-forward model and is grouped with the classical detectors. Each model consumes the
nine observables of Section~\ref{sec:features}. The fourteen span the classifier families
used across the cited detection literature of Section~\ref{sec:related}: tree ensembles
and boosting, instance-based classification, kernel methods, a shallow feed-forward
network, and, among the deep models, the convolutional, recurrent, hybrid and
attention-based branches of sequence modelling.

The support-vector machine uses a radial-basis-function kernel, matching the model
family \citet{an2025adversarial} attack, so their SVM configuration is represented
under the present protocol. Tuned by the same five-fold grid search as every other
classical model, it reaches a clean $F_1$ of 0.682, the weakest of the fourteen
detectors. A linear-kernel variant, trained on the same corpus for comparison,
separates the classes far worse and is not carried forward: a detector that does not
separate the classes to begin with is not a meaningful adversarial-robustness test.

\subsection{Common operating point}
\label{sec:oppoint}

A detector's recall, false-alarm rate and $F_1$ all depend on its decision threshold,
so a comparison drawn at different thresholds confounds the detector with its operating
point. We fix one common operating point for every model,
\begin{equation}
\eta^\star = \max\{\eta : R_\mathrm{val}(\eta)\ge 0.95\},
\label{eq:oppoint}
\end{equation}
the highest threshold whose recall $R_\mathrm{val}(\eta)$ on the clean validation block
still meets a floor of 0.95, which is the safety-first choice for an integrity monitor.
All clean and adversarial metrics are reported at $\eta^\star$. Because the false-alarm
rate at this recall floor is informative in its own right, we also report a second
operating point defined by a clean-validation false-alarm ceiling of 0.05, so that a
high false-alarm rate at the recall floor is visible rather than hidden by the choice
of threshold.

\subsection{Attack suite}
\label{sec:attacks}

Every detector is attacked by one shared suite at $\eta^\star$. The white-box attacks
are the single-step fast gradient sign method \citep{goodfellow2015explaining} and
iterative projected gradient descent \citep{madry2018towards}, both bounded by the
$L_\infty$ budget $\varepsilon$ and applied to the differentiable deep detectors. For
the non-differentiable classical detectors the
gradient attacks are transferred from deep surrogates, both from a single
convolutional surrogate and from a mean-logit ensemble of three surrogates, since an
ensemble of surrogates transfers more strongly than one
\citep{papernot2016transferability,liu2017delving}.

The suite also includes three GNSS domain attacks. We adapt the data location shift
attack and the similarity noise attack of \citet{an2025adversarial}, which were defined
in the reported-position domain against a single support vector machine, to the
receiver-observable domain, and we add a temporal pattern attack that modulates the
Doppler and $C/N_0$ observables. These attacks are heuristic and physically motivated
rather than optimised against the detector.

The decisive element of the suite is a gradient-free decision-based boundary attack
applied to every detector. For each correctly detected spoofed sample it searches, in
the min-max normalised observable space, for the smallest $L_\infty$ perturbation that
moves the sample across the decision boundary to the authentic side at $\eta^\star$,
\begin{equation}
\rho(\mathbf{x}) = \min_{\bm{\delta}}\ \lVert\bm{\delta}\rVert_\infty
\quad\text{subject to}\quad
s\!\big(\Pi_\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}+\bm{\delta})\big) < \eta^\star ,
\label{eq:fragility}
\end{equation}
the minimal-perturbation objective of \citet{szegedy2014intriguing} solved with
hard-label queries in the manner of \citet{brendel2018decision}, evaluated on
realizable examples through the enforcer $\Pi_\mathcal{F}$ of
Section~\ref{sec:enforcer}. The per-sample minimum $\rho(\mathbf{x})$ is the fragility
measure, and a detector's fragility is the median of $\rho$ over its correctly detected
spoofed samples: a smaller median means a smaller perturbation suffices, so the detector
is more fragile. Because the attack uses only the hard decision, it
applies uniformly to differentiable and non-differentiable detectors and provides the
matched measure of vulnerability that a transferred gradient attack cannot. The suite
characterises $L_\infty$ vulnerability; other norms such as $L_2$ or $L_0$
\citep{carlini2017evaluating} may expose different weaknesses and are outside the scope
of this study.

\subsection{Signal-validity enforcer}
\label{sec:enforcer}

Every adversarial example, at both training and evaluation, is projected onto the set
$\mathcal{F}$ of physically attainable receiver states,
$\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=\Pi_\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}+\bm{\delta})$, by a signal-validity
enforcer fitted on the training partition. Each observable is first clipped to a
conservative data-driven band. The enforcer then
couples the reported $C/N_0$ to the correlator power: it fits the log-linear relation
$\widehat{C/N_0}=a+b\log_{10}(I_P^2+Q_P^2)$ by least squares on the training partition
and admits only reported values within the empirical residual band of that prediction,
\begin{equation}
q_{1} \le (C/N_0) - \big(a + b\log_{10}(I_P^2+Q_P^2)\big) \le q_{99},
\label{eq:coupling}
\end{equation}
where $q_{1}$ and $q_{99}$ are the first and ninety-ninth percentiles of the training
residuals. An attacker therefore cannot raise the reported signal quality without also
supplying the correlator power that would produce it. This encodes the dominant physical
relation between the spoofer's amplitude control and the reported observables and is
what separates a constrained attack from an arbitrary feature perturbation.
Figure~\ref{fig:realizability} illustrates the effect. An attack that raises the reported
$C/N_0$ without supplying the correlator power that would produce it leaves the attainable
band, and the enforcer projects it back onto that band.

By construction the enforcer acts per epoch: it fixes the amplitude-to-power coupling
and the conservative per-observable bounds at the instant an observation is reported,
and it leaves the relation between successive epochs unconstrained. A transmitted
signal must also satisfy constraints the per-epoch view does not see, among them
carrier-phase and code-phase continuity, Doppler and clock dynamics, tracking-loop
(PLL and DLL) bandwidth and transient behaviour, automatic-gain-control response,
satellite geometry, and the epoch-to-epoch smoothness of the amplitude, phase and code
trajectories. An attack that respects the amplitude-power coupling can still fail one
of these, so a temporally aware receiver that checks carrier-tracking-loop consistency
across epochs could reject a signal the present enforcer admits. The reported attacks
are accordingly bounded by the single-epoch coupling that most directly governs a
feature-space perturbation, not certified against every constraint a real signal must
meet; adding the remaining constraints, particularly the temporal smoothness of the
amplitude, phase and code trajectories across epochs, would tighten rather than loosen
the attacker's feasible set, and is the natural next layer of the threat model.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol and statistics}
\label{sec:protocol}

Detection is measured under three views. The primary view is the block-temporal split
within the mixed corpus. The cross-scenario view holds out one spoofing scenario
entirely and trains on the remainder, which measures generalization to an unseen attack
type. The leave-satellite view holds out one satellite, which measures generalization
across satellites. Every fold sets the common operating point on its own validation
block and retrains each detector on its own training block, so no information from the
held-out fold enters training.

Each deep detector is trained over three random seeds; the standard deviation of clean
test $F_1$ across seeds is at most 0.024 and of AUC at most 0.019, so the deep-model
results are stable to retraining. The decision-based fragility is measured on one
representative trained model per architecture, since the boundary attack is run per
sample and per detector; the effect sizes that carry the fragility ranking span roughly
sixfold (Section~\ref{sec:res_adv}), well beyond the across-seed clean variation, so the
ordering is unlikely to be an artifact of a single training run, though a full
seed-by-attack sweep is left to future work.

The decision-based attack is evaluated on 500 correctly detected spoofed test epochs per
detector, drawn as a uniform random sample from the roughly nineteen thousand such epochs
in the test partition, so the sample is representative of the test scenario mix rather
than stratified by scenario; a fixed sample size keeps the per-detector attack cost
comparable, and per-scenario fragility is not separately resolved at this size.

The effect of the fixed-budget attacks is summarised by the attack success rate, the
fraction of initially correct detections that an attack flips at $\eta^\star$.
Uncertainty on the clean $F_1$ is reported as a 10{,}000-sample bootstrap confidence
interval, and the median minimum $L_\infty$ perturbation of each detector is reported
with a 10{,}000-sample bootstrap interval over the attacked samples. Pairwise detector
differences, computed on the clean test-set decisions, are tested with the mid-$p$
McNemar test \citep{dietterich1998approximate} under Holm correction across all pairs,
and heterogeneity across attacks is tested with the Friedman test
\citep{demsar2006statistical}. Reported timings are per-sample inference and
attack-generation latency; training cost ranged from seconds for the tree detectors to
tens of minutes for the deep detectors and the kernel machine on the run hardware, and
memory footprint was not profiled.
