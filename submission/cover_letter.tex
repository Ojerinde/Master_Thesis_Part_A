\noindent Dear Editor,

\vspace{1em}

\noindent We submit our manuscript, ``Adversarial Vulnerability of Machine Learning GNSS
Spoofing Detectors to Transmitted Spoofing Signals,'' for consideration as an Original
Article in \emph{Satellite Navigation}.

\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{Relationship to a previous submission}

\vspace{0.5em}

\noindent An earlier manuscript by the same authors on learned spoofing detectors
(SANA-D-26-00339) was declined by \emph{Satellite Navigation} in September 2026. We
submit the present work as a new manuscript rather than a revision, because the study it
reports is different in method and in evidence.

The earlier submission evaluated detectors by perturbing receiver observables directly and
argued separately that the resulting perturbations were physically attainable. The
reviewer identified that argument as the weak point of the study. In the present work the
attack is constructed as a radio waveform: a counterfeit GPS L1 C/A signal is synthesised
at sample level, summed with authentic recordings, and tracked by an open software
receiver, so the observables each detector reads are produced by receiver processing of a
transmittable signal. The question of realizability is therefore settled by the
construction of the experiment rather than by an argument attached to it. The results,
the figures and the conclusions are new.

\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{Why this manuscript belongs in \emph{Satellite Navigation}}

\vspace{0.5em}

\noindent Machine learning spoofing detectors are moving from research prototypes towards
integration in receivers, and their reported accuracy is obtained against fixed corpora of
recorded attacks that do not respond to the detector under test. This manuscript evaluates
fourteen detectors, eight classical and six deep, against 126 transmitter settings that
satisfy the published conditions for receiver takeover, with every detector held at a
common operating point and the false alarm rate reported alongside every result.

Three results follow. Clean detection performance predicts resistance poorly, and at the
top of the ranking the two orderings are close to inverted. Where resistance appears it is
purchased with false alarm rates of 46 to 74 percent on authentic data, which is not
operationally available. A missed attack propagates to the navigation solution, driving
the receiver clock at 48.89 nanoseconds per second against a commanded 48.87.

The work addresses resilient positioning, navigation and timing, a core topic of the
journal, and connects directly to the literature on learning based GNSS spoofing detection
the journal has published. The library of transmitted attacks is a reusable artefact for
evaluating physics constrained and certified detectors.
