\noindent Dear Editor,

\vspace{1em}

\noindent We submit our manuscript, ``Adversarial Vulnerability of Machine Learning GNSS
Spoofing Detectors to Transmitted Spoofing Signals,'' for consideration as an Original
Article in \emph{Satellite Navigation}.

\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{The problem the manuscript addresses}

\vspace{0.5em}

\noindent Machine learning spoofing detectors are moving from research prototypes towards
integration in receivers, and the accuracies reported for them are high. Those accuracies
are obtained against fixed corpora of recorded attacks. A recorded attack does not respond
to the detector being tested, so what such an evaluation establishes is performance against
the attacks someone else already chose to record. A deployed receiver faces an adversary
who selects what to transmit.

Closing that gap requires the attack to be a signal. Work that perturbs receiver
observables directly must argue separately that the perturbation corresponds to something
a transmitter could produce, and the argument is difficult to make because several
observables are computed from the same correlator accumulations within one integration
period: displacing one while holding the others fixed specifies a receiver state no
incident waveform produces.

\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{What we did}

\vspace{0.5em}

\noindent Every attack in this study is constructed as a radio waveform. A counterfeit
GPS L1 C/A signal is synthesised at sample level, summed with authentic recordings, passed
through a model of the receiver front end and tracked by an open software receiver, so the
observables each detector reads are produced by receiver processing of a transmittable
signal. Realizability is settled by the construction of the experiment, not by an argument
attached to it. The generator is validated by reproducing a recorded attack whose
construction is published.

We sweep 209 transmitter settings spanning counterfeit power, code pull-off rate and
carrier alignment, of which 126 satisfy the published conditions for receiver takeover, and
measure fourteen detectors, eight classical and six deep, against that subset. Every
detector is held at one common operating point, and the false alarm rate each pays is
reported beside every result, so no apparent robustness is credited to a displaced
threshold.

\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{What we found}

\vspace{0.5em}

\noindent Clean detection performance is a poor predictor of resistance to transmitted
attacks, and near the top of the ranking the two orderings run close to inverted. The
detector with the highest clean $F_1$ and the lowest false alarm rate fails to flag 55 of
the 126 attacks. Where resistance does appear it is purchased with false alarm rates of 46
to 74 percent on authentic data, which is not operationally available to a receiver. And
detection failure is not confined to the classifier: following one missed attack through
the navigation stage, the receiver clock is driven at 48.89 nanoseconds per second against
a commanded 48.87.

\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{Why \emph{Satellite Navigation}}

\vspace{0.5em}

\noindent The manuscript addresses resilient positioning, navigation and timing, a core
topic of the journal, and connects directly to the literature on learning based GNSS
spoofing detection that the journal has published. Its findings are stated in the terms a
receiver designer must weigh, namely detection against false alarms and the consequence of
a miss for the position and timing solution. The library of transmitted attacks is a
reusable artefact for evaluating physics constrained and certified detectors, which we
expect to be of direct use to the journal's readership.
